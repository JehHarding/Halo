Is Your Dog Overweight?
How to Tell

4 min read

A dog does not have to look round to be carrying too much. Here is the quick check vets use, which you can do at home.

A dog does not have to look round to be carrying too much. Fur hides a lot, and when you see your dog every day, small changes are easy to miss. The good news is there is a quick check you can do at home in under a minute, with nothing but your hands and your eyes.

The hands-on check

Vets and charities use something called a body condition score, on a scale of 1 to 9. A score of 4 to 5 is the healthy range. You can get a good idea of where your dog sits in three steps.

  • Feel the ribs. Run your hands along your dog's sides. You should be able to feel the ribs easily with a light touch, under a thin layer of padding. If you have to press hard to find them, there may be too much cover.
  • Look from above. Stand over your dog. There should be a visible waist, a gentle narrowing behind the ribs. If your dog looks straight or bulging from above, that is worth noticing.
  • Look from the side. The belly should tuck up towards the back legs. A belly that hangs level, or lower than the chest, can mean extra weight.
Thick coats can fool you. On a fluffy or long-haired dog, feel is far more reliable than looks. Press gently through the fur, and trust your fingers.

What the scale can and cannot tell you

The scale matters, but it is only half the picture. A number on its own cannot tell you how much of that weight is fat and how much is muscle or frame, and breed ranges vary a lot. What helps is how your dog's weight changes over time, together with the hands-on check.

How to weigh your dog

  • For small dogs, a bathroom scale works. Weigh yourself, then weigh yourself holding your dog, and take one from the other.
  • For bigger dogs, many vet practices and pet shops have a scale you can use for free. It is worth asking.
  • Weigh at roughly the same time of day, and about once a month.

If you think your dog is overweight

Talk to your vet before changing their food or exercise, especially for older dogs or dogs with joint or health problems. They can confirm the score, rule out other causes, and suggest a plan that suits your dog. Cutting back suddenly on your own can do more harm than good.

This guide is for information only and isn't veterinary advice. If you are worried about your dog's weight, or they are losing weight without trying, speak to your vet.
