Compounded Medications:
What They Are, and When They Help

4 min read

For a cat who simply will not take a pill, no matter the method, there's often an option that doesn't get mentioned until someone asks directly: a compounding pharmacy can frequently turn the same medication into something far easier to give.

What compounding actually is

A compounding pharmacy reformulates an existing medication, often into a flavoured liquid, a transdermal gel absorbed through the skin, or a smaller, easier-to-swallow capsule. It's the same active medication, just in a different, sometimes far more manageable form.

When it's actually worth asking about

  • A cat who consistently refuses or fights every pilling method tried
  • A medication that needs splitting into an unusually small or awkward dose
  • A multi-pet household where dosing needs to be extremely precise per pet
Worth knowing: not every medication can be safely compounded, and not every compounding pharmacy produces equally reliable results. This is worth doing through your vet's recommended pharmacy, not an unverified one.

What to actually ask your vet

Simply: "is there a compounded version of this available, and would it suit her?" It's a fair, reasonable question, and one a lot of owners never think to ask until pilling has already become a real ongoing struggle.

Whatever the format, the tracking stays the same

A switch to a compounded version doesn't change what needs recording, dose, time, and whether it was given. Halo tracks the schedule itself, independent of exactly how a dose physically goes in, alongside the same options covered in pill pockets vs syringe vs crushed food.

Log it the same way, however it's given

Halo tracks the dose and schedule, not the delivery method, so switching formats never means losing the record.
