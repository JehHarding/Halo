Weighing a Great Dane
vs a Guinea Pig:
Why the Method Changes

4 min read

A 50 gram change means nothing on a Great Dane and everything on a guinea pig. Getting useful weight data isn't about owning a fancy scale, it's about matching the method, and the units, to the animal.

Weight is one of the single most useful health metrics for any pet, but the right way to measure it, and how much a small change should actually worry you, depends entirely on scale. A method that works well for a cat is useless for a horse, and vice versa.

Matching the method to the pet

Pet sizeBest methodMeaningful change
Small pets (rabbit, guinea pig, bird)Kitchen or baby scale, gramsAs little as 2–5%
Cats & small dogsHold-and-subtract on bathroom scale, or a pet scaleAround 5%
Large dogsDedicated platform scale, most vets have one free to useAround 5%
HorsesWeighbridge, or a weigh tape for an estimateAround 5% of bodyweight

The hold-and-subtract method, done properly

For cats and small dogs without access to a pet scale, weigh yourself alone on a bathroom scale, then weigh yourself holding the pet, and subtract. It's not perfectly precise, but done consistently, at the same time of day, on the same scale, it's genuinely useful for tracking trends over time, which matters more than any single exact number.

Worth knowing: for very small pets, a kitchen scale accurate to single grams matters far more than it sounds like it would. A guinea pig losing 50 grams is losing around 5% of their bodyweight, the same proportional change as a 25kg dog losing over a kilo.

Why consistency matters more than precision

Weighing at the same time of day, ideally before feeding, and on the same scale each time, removes most of the noise that makes single readings hard to interpret. A trend across several weigh-ins tells you far more than any one number in isolation, whatever animal you're weighing.

This guide is for information only and isn't veterinary advice. Every pet is different, always ask your vet what a meaningful weight change looks like for your own pet's size and breed.

Where the numbers actually go
