Tracking a Horse's
Vital Signs at Home

5 min read

A vet on the phone will always ask the same four things first: heart rate, respiratory rate, temperature, and gut sounds. Knowing how to check them, and what's actually normal for your own horse, changes how fast that call gets answered.

None of these checks require special equipment beyond a stethoscope and a watch, and all four are worth practising when your horse is well, not for the first time during an emergency. Knowing your horse's own resting baseline matters more than knowing the textbook average, since individual horses vary within the normal range.

The four vitals, and how to check them

VitalNormal rangeHow to check
Heart rate28–44 bpm at restStethoscope behind the left elbow, or feel the pulse under the jaw
Respiratory rate8–16 breaths/minWatch the flank rise and fall, or feel breath at the nostril
Temperature37.2–38.3°CDigital thermometer, rectal, lubricated
Gut soundsRegular gurgling, all four quadrantsStethoscope on the flank, both sides, listen a full minute per side

Why the trend matters more than one reading

A single normal reading tells you very little on its own if you don't know what's normal for that specific horse. A heart rate of 40 might be entirely typical for one horse and a real warning sign for another whose resting rate usually sits at 28. This is exactly why regularly checking and recording vitals when a horse is healthy is what makes the numbers useful later.

Worth knowing: gut sounds are one of the most useful early warning signs for colic. A quiet or silent gut, especially combined with any other colic sign, is worth calling your vet about immediately rather than waiting to see if sounds return.

What actually changes vet response time

A vet triaging a phone call about a possibly unwell horse can make a much faster, more accurate decision if you can tell them exact numbers rather than "he seems a bit off." Heart rate especially is one of the clearest indicators of pain and distress severity, and being able to report it accurately, along with how it compares to your horse's known normal, genuinely changes how quickly the right response happens.

This guide is for information only and isn't veterinary advice. Every horse is different, always ask your vet what's normal for your own horse, and contact them directly if you're ever concerned.
