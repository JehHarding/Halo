Bonfire Night: How to
Keep Any Pet Calm

5 min read

For a lot of animals, the fireworks around 5 November are the most frightening nights of the year. A little planning now makes it far easier for any pet, big or small.

Quick answer
Plan ahead: walk dogs in daylight, keep cats and small pets safely in, make a quiet hiding place, and talk to your vet early if your pet struggles every year.

Fireworks are not just loud, they are unpredictable. A pet has no idea what the bangs are, when they will stop, or whether they are in danger. That is why Bonfire Night is one of the hardest nights of the year for animals, and why a little planning in the days before makes such a difference.

This applies to nearly every pet, not just dogs. Cats, rabbits, guinea pigs, birds and horses all feel it, and the small ones are easy to forget.

How stress shows up

Pets do not always look dramatic when they are frightened. Common signs include:

  • Shaking, panting or pacing
  • Hiding, or pressing themselves into a corner
  • Barking, crying or other unusual noise
  • Going to the toilet indoors
  • Refusing food or treats
  • Chewing or scratching things they normally leave alone
  • In rabbits, stamping a back foot or sitting completely frozen

Freezing is easy to mistake for calm. A rabbit or small pet that has gone very still and quiet is not necessarily relaxed.

Plan ahead, not on the night

Fireworks are often let off for days either side of 5 November, so it is worth getting set up early.

  • Walk dogs in daylight. Get exercise and toilet trips done before it gets dark.
  • Keep cats in. Many charities suggest keeping them indoors for the evenings around Bonfire Night, with the cat flap locked so they cannot bolt outside.
  • Check the microchip. Make sure the details are current and your pet is wearing ID. If one does escape, this is what gets them home.
  • Make a hiding place. A den made with an old jumper or blanket, somewhere quiet like under a bed, gives them a place that feels safe. Let them use it and do not drag them out.
  • Close curtains and windows. It softens both the flashes and the noise.
  • Add background sound. A radio or the television on at a normal, quiet volume can take the edge off. Do not turn it up loud.
Stay calm yourself: if you act normally, your pet is more likely to take their cue from you. Reassure them if they come to you, but there is no need to make a fuss, and never tell them off for being scared.

Small pets, birds and horses

Rabbits, guinea pigs and other small animals living outdoors need extra thought, because they cannot hide or be walked away from the noise.

  • Bring hutches and cages indoors if you can, or into a quiet garage or shed.
  • If they have to stay outside, turn the hutch so the front faces a wall or fence rather than the open garden.
  • Give them plenty of extra bedding so they can burrow.
  • Partly cover the hutch or cage with a thick blanket, but always leave enough ventilation so air can still move through.

For birds, a quiet room and a light cover over part of the cage can help them feel more secure. For horses and ponies, ask neighbours and local organisers when displays are planned, so you can talk to your yard about the best plan for stabling or turnout well in advance.

If your pet is already scared of fireworks

Firework fear can usually be helped, and the earlier you start the better. If your pet is badly affected every year, speak to your vet well before the night. They can talk through options, which may include calming products or a referral to a clinical animal behaviourist. Do not give any human medication.

This guide is for information only and isn't veterinary advice. If your pet is panting heavily and cannot settle, is struggling to breathe, or has hurt themselves, call your vet.

Do not skip the routine

If your pet takes regular medication, the evening dose can easily land in the middle of the noise. It is still important, so give it in a quiet room away from the windows and do not let the evening throw out the routine. If they have a heart or breathing condition, ask your vet beforehand what to watch for on the night, and what is normal for them afterwards.

Write down how it went

Once the evening is over, take a minute to note how your pet coped. What helped, what they did, how long they took to settle. Halo's daily log is a handy place for it, and it means next year you are not trying to remember. If you do need to speak to your vet about fireworks fear, a few specific notes are far more useful than "they were a bit upset".
