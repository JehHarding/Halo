Why Dogs Hide Pain
Differently Than Cats

4 min read

Cats hide pain almost completely, a survival instinct from being both predator and prey. Dogs are more social and more expressive, which is exactly why a dog who's genuinely struggling can still slip past unnoticed.

Dogs evolved as pack animals that relied on group cohesion, so they didn't develop the same total pain-masking instinct cats did. That doesn't mean dogs are easy to read, though. A dog in pain often keeps doing the things that matter most to them, greeting you, wagging, eating, right up until the pain is fairly advanced, because the drive to stay socially connected is strong.

The "still wagging" trap

This is the single most common way owners miss pain in dogs. A dog can be in genuine discomfort and still wag their tail when you walk in, still eat, still want attention. Tail wagging reflects social engagement, not comfort level. It's entirely possible for both things to be true at once.

What actually shows up first

  • Subtle changes in how they move, a slightly shorter stride, hesitating before jumping, taking stairs one at a time instead of two
  • Panting at rest, when it's not hot and they haven't been exercising
  • Restlessness, or the opposite, sleeping noticeably more and initiating play less
  • Changes in posture, a hunched back, a lowered head carriage, weight shifted off one leg
  • Grumpiness with familiar handling, a dog who tolerated grooming or touch before suddenly not wanting to
Worth knowing: breed and personality both affect how visibly a dog shows pain. Stoic working breeds in particular are known for masking discomfort far longer than their size or history would suggest, right up until it's fairly severe.

The cat comparison, briefly

Cats hide pain almost totally as a species-wide instinct, so even small behaviour changes in a cat are usually worth real attention. Dogs sit somewhere in between, expressive enough that owners often assume they'd "obviously know" if something was wrong, which is exactly the assumption that lets early pain slip through unnoticed.

This guide is for information only and isn't veterinary advice. Every pet is different, always ask your vet what's normal for your own pet, and contact them directly if you're ever worried.

Why the baseline matters more than any single sign

None of these signs mean much on their own, a dog can pant, hesitate, or sleep more for a hundred harmless reasons. What actually matters is knowing what's normal for your own dog well enough to notice a genuine shift.
