How to Count Your Cat's Resting Breathing Rate
(And Why It Matters)

6 min read

Angel, the cat behind Halo, has four heart conditions. At every single appointment, her cardiologist asked the same question: "Have you been counting her breaths at home?" For years, the honest answer was no, not properly, not consistently, not in a way anyone trusted enough to repeat back to them.

It turns out this 15-second check is one of the earliest, cheapest, most reliable ways to catch a worsening heart or lung condition before it becomes an emergency. It costs nothing, takes less time than reading this sentence, and most pet owners have never been shown how to actually do it properly. So here's exactly how.

What is a resting respiratory rate?

It's simply how many breaths your cat takes in a minute while they're completely at rest, not sleeping deeply, not just after playing, not stressed at the vet. One full breath is one rise and fall of the chest or belly.

Vets care about this number specifically because it tends to creep up before other, more obvious symptoms of heart or respiratory disease show up. A slow, steady rise over days or weeks is often the first real warning sign, well before a cat looks or acts unwell.

The exact method

  1. Wait for the right moment. Your cat needs to be fully relaxed or asleep, not just lying still while alert. A tense or watchful cat breathes faster than their true resting rate.
  2. Watch the flank, not the chest. The easiest place to see the rise and fall is the soft area just behind the ribs. One full rise and fall counts as one breath.
  3. Count for 15 seconds. Use a timer, don't estimate. Count every complete breath you see in that window.
  4. Multiply by four. That gives you breaths per minute, the number your vet actually wants.
Tip: do this at roughly the same time of day, in the same relaxed setting, so you're comparing like for like. A single reading tells you very little. A trend over weeks tells you almost everything.

What counts as normal?

For most cats, these are the general bands vets work from:

Breaths per minuteWhat it means
30 or underNormal
30 to 40Watch closely, mention it to your vet
Over 40Call your vet promptly

Angel's own baseline actually runs a little higher than "textbook normal", usually 28 to 38 breaths per minute, because of her existing heart conditions. That's exactly why the trend matters more than any single number: what's normal for your pet might not match the general guide, and your vet needs your pet's own baseline to judge a real change.

When it's time to call the vet

  • A reading consistently above 40, even at rest
  • A steady upward trend over several days, even if each individual reading still seems "fine"
  • Breathing that looks laboured, open-mouthed, or effortful, alongside the rate itself
  • Any sudden, sharp change from your pet's own normal baseline
This guide is for information only and isn't veterinary advice. Every pet is different, always ask your vet about what's normal for your own pet, and contact them directly if you're ever worried.
