# Vet outreach

Drafted 2026-08-13. Two emails, in the order they should be sent.

## Why this is shaped as a feedback request, not a pitch

Vets get pitched constantly and ignore almost all of it. They do answer clinical curiosity, and they answer clients. So neither email below asks anyone to recommend, promote or endorse Halo. Both ask for an opinion.

That is not a trick, it is the genuinely useful thing to ask for first. You do not yet have a single vet who has looked at Halo and said it makes sense, and until you do, every downstream claim you might want to make is unsupported. One vet's sentence unlocks the next twenty emails.

The second reason: if a cardiologist tells you the PDF is missing something they would need, that is worth more than a download.

## Email 1: your own vet

Send this first, and on its own. You are a real client with a real patient, which no cold email can buy.

**Subject:** A quick favour, and something I built for Angel

```
Hi [name],

You have looked after Angel through all four of her heart conditions, so
you know better than anyone how much of her care happens between
appointments.

I am a software developer, and over the last year I built an app to keep
on top of hers: medications, resting breathing rate, weight, and a record
of every appointment. It started as something just for us. It is now on
the App Store.

I am not asking you to recommend it. What I would really value is five
minutes of your honest opinion on two things. Whether it is clinically
sensible, and whether the summary it produces is something you would
actually find useful if a client handed it to you at a consult.

It is free, and it deliberately makes no diagnostic or treatment claims,
it is a record keeping tool and nothing more. If you would rather look at
a PDF than install anything, I can send you the summary it generates for
Angel.

Either way, thank you for everything you have done for her.

Jehane
myhaloapp.co.uk
```

**What to do with the answer.** If it is positive, ask one follow-up: "would you be comfortable with me quoting that?" A single named vet saying Halo is sensible changes the tone of every email in the next section.

## Email 2: cardiology referral practices

Practice list and priority order in `PRACTICE-LIST.md`.

**Subject:** A digital alternative to the SRR monitoring booklet

```
Dear [name or Cardiology Service],

You send owners of newly diagnosed heart failure patients home with a
booklet to record sleeping respiratory rate, and ask them to bring it to
the recheck. In practice a lot of those booklets get lost, filled in from
memory the night before, or abandoned after a fortnight.

I am the owner of a cat with four heart conditions, and I am also a
software developer. I built Halo to solve that problem for myself. It
records SRR, medications and weight, charts the trend over time, and
exports a one page summary the owner can bring in or email ahead of a
recheck.

I am not asking you to endorse or recommend anything. I would like to know
whether a cardiologist would find that summary useful, and whether there
is anything in it you would want changed before it were ever put in front
of your clients. If the answer is that it is not useful, that is genuinely
worth me knowing.

It is free, there is no account or sign up, and it makes no diagnostic or
treatment claims. I am happy to send a sample PDF rather than ask you to
install anything.

Kind regards,
Jehane Harding
Halo, myhaloapp.co.uk
hello@myhaloapp.co.uk
```

### If someone replies positively

Do not immediately ask for promotion. Ask in this order:

1. "Would you be comfortable with me quoting that?"
2. "Is there anything you would want changed?" Then actually change it, and tell them you did. This is the step that turns a polite reply into a relationship.
3. Only then: "Would you be willing to mention it on your home monitoring page, or to clients you send home with a booklet?"

Offer free Halo Plus codes for their clients at step 3, not before. App Store Connect can generate promo codes, and it costs you nothing.

## Rules for sending

- **Personalise the first line every time.** Reference their actual home monitoring page or their named cardiologist. A visibly templated email to a specialist reads as spam and burns the contact permanently.
- **Send in batches of five, a week apart.** Not sixty at once. You want to learn from the first replies and change the email before it goes to the rest.
- **Never BCC a group.**
- **Get the address from each practice's own contact page.** Do not guess an address format, and do not use a general enquiries inbox where a named cardiology contact exists.
- **Use hello@myhaloapp.co.uk**, not a personal Gmail.

### One compliance note

Under UK PECR, unsolicited marketing email to a corporate subscriber, meaning a limited company or LLP, is allowed without prior consent. Sole traders and ordinary partnerships count as individual subscribers and are not. Plenty of independent vet practices are partnerships, so this is not a blanket permission.

Two things keep you comfortably on the right side of it. These emails are a request for professional opinion rather than a solicitation to buy, and Halo is free. Always identify yourself properly and honour any request to stop, which the template above already does by signing with a real name and a real address.

This is a practical note, not legal advice. If you ever scale this past a few dozen personalised emails, it is worth a proper look.

## A terminology point, smaller than I first thought

I checked the app's actual strings. Halo already says **"Respiratory Rate"** throughout the interface, including "Log a resting respiratory rate" on the setup card and "Respiratory rate check" in notifications. The only place "breathing" appears in user-facing copy is "Laboured breathing" as a symptom, which is the correct term there. So the app is already speaking the profession's language, and you can write to a cardiologist without worrying about this.

The one remaining nuance: cardiology tends to specify **sleeping** respiratory rate (SRR) rather than resting (RRR), because a sleeping measurement is less prone to being inflated by activity or stress. Halo says resting. That difference is real but minor, and worth a question rather than a change: it is a good thing to ask a cardiologist in the very first email, because it shows you care about the distinction.

This is also why "resting" and "respiratory" are both in the new App Store keyword field.
